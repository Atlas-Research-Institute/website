Recorded for Atlas fellows in summer 2026. Fuller is trained in sociology, has also taught at Harvard, and worked at the World Bank for several years. Atlas is not affiliated with or endorsed by these universities.

## Summary

Fuller walks through how a researcher gets from a problem they care about to evidence that can actually answer a question. He describes research as shared work: each study adds a small piece to a large body of evidence and is judged by whether it confirms or disconfirms what others have found. Using examples from preschool and from AI in classrooms, he separates three kinds of questions (describing, correlating and explaining) and shows why a correlation is not yet a cause. He ends on how to present findings honestly when the people around you hold strong beliefs about the topic.

## Key takeaways

1. **Describing, correlating and explaining are different jobs.** Describing says what is there: how preschools vary in class size or teacher experience. Correlating says what goes with what: do preschools that spend more money have smaller classes? Explaining makes a causal claim, and that needs much stronger evidence. Fuller says even his PhD students mix these up.
2. **Watch for confounds.** Children in smaller preschool classes may learn more, but children from better-off families are also more likely to be in smaller classes. Their background could be driving both. A randomized experiment, or a quasi-experimental design when random assignment isn't possible, is how researchers pull the effect of class size apart from who ends up in small classes.
3. **Ask for whom, and under what conditions.** An effect can hold in one setting and not another (scope conditions), and it can help some groups more than others (heterogeneous effects). Preschool may lift learning for some children and do little for others; AI may help some students' writing and not others'.
4. **Separate beliefs from testable questions.** "Everyone benefits from preschool" and "AI is a waste of time" are positions people hold with conviction. Research respects them, but breaks them into small questions that evidence can answer.
